## What is Mastering, and How Does it Differ from Mixing?

Mastering and mixing are two distinct stages in the music production process. Mixing involves blending multiple tracks to create a balanced sound, while mastering prepares the final mixed audio for distribution across various platforms. The goal of mastering is to make the music sound its best on different speakers, headphones, and playback systems.

## The Mastering Process: A Step-by-Step Explanation

1. **File Preparation**: Ensure your files are prepared according to the technical requirements outlined in our [Technical FAQ](/technical-faq#mix-prep). This includes uploading your mixed stems, metadata, and any additional artwork.
2. **Format Conversion**: Mastering engineers often work in high-resolution formats, such as 24-bit/96kHz WAV or AIFF. These formats allow for a wider dynamic range and more precise control over the final master.
3. **Equalization (EQ)**: The mastering engineer will apply EQ to balance the tone of the music, often adding or subtracting frequencies to create a more cohesive sound. This process is typically done in the range of 10-20 Hz for low-end enhancement, 200-500 Hz for midrange clarity, and 5-10 kHz for high-end sparkle.
4. **Compression**: Dynamic range compression (DRC) is applied to control the overall loudness of the music and maintain a consistent level. This is particularly important for punk records, which often feature a high-energy, driving sound.
5. **Stereo Imaging and Width**: By adjusting the stereo image and width, the mastering engineer can enhance the sense of space and clarity in the mix. This can involve creating a wider soundstage, adding depth, or adjusting the interaural differences.
6. **Limiting and Clipping**: To prevent distortion and ensure compatibility with various playback systems, the mastering engineer will apply limiting and clipping techniques to the final master. This process involves preventing the audio signal from exceeding a certain amplitude, thereby maintaining a safe headroom.
7. **Dither and Noise Shaping**: Dither is a low-level noise that's added to the audio signal to reduce quantization errors. Noise shaping involves adjusting the frequency response of the dither to minimize its audible impact.
